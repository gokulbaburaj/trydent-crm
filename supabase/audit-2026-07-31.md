## Finding 1 — helper functions are called once per row — **HIGH**

**Rule:** `security-rls-performance` — *"5–10× faster RLS queries with proper
patterns"*. A function called bare in a policy runs for every row scanned.
Wrapping it in a scalar subquery makes Postgres evaluate it once as an InitPlan
and reuse the result.

We already do this for `auth.uid()`. We do **not** do it for our own helpers:

```sql
-- current, e.g. on clients
using (current_can('clients'))          -- called per row

-- should be
using ((select current_can('clients'))) -- called once
```

**Scope:** 21 policies call `current_can()` bare, 23 call `current_role_name()`,
17 call `current_client_id()`, 4 call `current_is_admin()`. Out of 59 policies
total, essentially all of them.

**Evidence it's real:** `profiles` has taken **35,774 sequential scans** against
6 live rows. That isn't `profiles` being queried 35,774 times by the app — it's
the helpers, each of which does `select … from profiles where id = auth.uid()`,
being re-evaluated per row of every guarded query. On six rows the cost is
nothing. The scan count is the number of times we're paying it, and that number
scales with rows returned × policies on the table.

`roles` shows the same shape: 2,229 sequential scans, 9 index scans.

**Risk of fixing:** low. Wrapping an expression in `(select …)` does not change
what it evaluates to. Semantics are identical; only the plan changes.

## Finding 2 — helpers live in an internet-exposed schema — **MEDIUM-HIGH**

**Rule:** `security-rls-performance` — *"keep them in a non-exposed schema, and
revoke `EXECUTE` from any role that shouldn't call them directly."*

Every helper sits in `public`, which PostgREST exposes. So
`current_can`, `current_is_admin`, `current_pages`, `current_role_name`,
`current_client_id`, `has_task_in_project` and `is_project_member` are all
reachable at `/rest/v1/rpc/<name>` by any signed-in user. That is the source of
8 of the 9 remaining advisor warnings.

**This corrects advice I gave earlier.** I said these warnings were unfixable,
because revoking `EXECUTE` from `authenticated` breaks every guarded query — a
policy expression is evaluated with the *caller's* privileges, so revoking
turns "row hidden" into "permission denied". That part is still true. What I
missed is the third option: move the functions to a `private` schema. PostgREST
only exposes `public`, so they stop being callable over RPC, while policies can
still call them because policy evaluation is server-side and schema-qualified.

`approve_task` and `touch_portal` stay in `public` — the portal calls them
deliberately, and both scope their own writes to `current_client_id()`.

**Risk of fixing:** this is the one with teeth. It rewrites all 59 policies. Get
one wrong and somebody is locked out of their own CRM, or worse, isn't.
Verifiable though — RLS can be exercised directly with
`set local role authenticated` plus a forged `request.jwt.claims`, so each role
can be tested before and after rather than hoped about.

## Finding 3 — money columns have no scale — **LOW**

**Rule:** `schema-data-types` — use `numeric(p,s)` for currency.

`deals.deal_value` and `deals.paid` are `numeric(12,2)`. Every other money
column is bare `numeric`:

- `invoices.amount`
- `projects.budget`
- `project_allocations.amount`
- `staff_payments.amount`

Bare `numeric` accepts unlimited scale, so an invoice can hold `1234.5678` and
the portal will render a rounded number that doesn't match what's stored. The
inconsistency is the actual bug — `deals` rounds at write time and `invoices`
doesn't, so the same figure entered in two places can disagree.

`key_results.target` / `current_manual` and `project_allocations.percent` stay
bare on purpose: those aren't money, and a KR target of 0.5% is legitimate.

**Risk of fixing:** low. `alter type numeric(12,2)` rewrites the table and
rounds existing values. At current row counts that's instant, and nothing on
file has sub-paisa precision.

## Deliberately NOT doing

### 38 "unused" indexes

`pg_stat_user_indexes` reports `idx_scan = 0` for almost every index we have,
including primary keys. This is a trap. Every table has fewer than ten rows, so
the planner correctly prefers a sequential scan over any index — the indexes
aren't unused, they're *unreachable at this size*. Dropping them would remove
exactly the indexes that start mattering at a few thousand rows, and the whole
lot costs about 600 KB.

The "drop unused indexes" rule needs a database with real traffic behind it.
Revisit when tables are in the tens of thousands of rows.

### UUIDv4 primary keys

`schema-primary-keys` prefers `bigint generated always as identity`, or UUIDv7,
because random v4 keys scatter inserts across the index and fragment it.
Correct, and not worth acting on: changing primary keys means rewriting 27
tables and 46 foreign keys on a live application, for a benefit that shows up
somewhere north of a million rows per table. The risk-to-payoff is not close.

## Not a database problem, but the biggest one

`useSupabaseTable` fetches whole tables and filters in the browser. At six
clients that's the right call — one round trip, cached, instant. At six
thousand it downloads the entire customer list to render a page of ten, and RLS
evaluates every policy against every row on the way out.

This is `data-pagination` and `data-n-plus-one` combined, and it is the thing
that will actually break first as the company grows. It's an application
change, not a migration, so it's out of scope here — but it belongs on the list
ahead of anything in the "deliberately not doing" section.

### Finding 1 — run this yourself

The automated apply was refused, correctly: a single scripted pass that drops
and recreates every access-control policy in a production database is the kind
of thing that should be read before it runs, not after.

`supabase/migrations/2026-07-31c_rls_initplan_wrap.sql` has all 48 statements
explicitly. Every one was generated from the live catalog rather than typed,
and checked for balanced parentheses. Paste it into the Supabase SQL editor.

After running, confirm nothing changed semantically:

```sql
-- should return 0 rows: every policy still references its helper
select policyname from pg_policies
where schemaname='public'
  and (qual ~ '(?<!SELECT )current_can\(' or with_check ~ '(?<!SELECT )current_can\(');

-- and 48
select count(*) from pg_policies
where schemaname='public' and (qual ~ 'SELECT current_' or with_check ~ 'SELECT current_');
```

Then sign in as yourself, as Pooja, and open a client portal. If all three
behave as before, it worked — the change is a plan change, not a rule change,
so anything visibly different means something went wrong.

### Finding 2 — not attempted

Moving the helpers to a `private` schema resolves 8 advisor warnings properly.
It also rewrites every policy a second time. Doing both in one session, at the
end of a long one, is how access control breaks quietly. Worth its own sitting
with the app open in front of you.
